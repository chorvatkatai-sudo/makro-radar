Short-Horizon Currency Expectations
Abstract
In this paper, we show that only the systematic component of exchange rate expectations
of professional investors is a strong predictor of the cross-section of currency returns.
The predictability is strong inshort and long horizons. The strategy offers significant
Sharpe ratios for holding periods of 1 to 12 months, and it is unrelated to existing
currency investment strategies, including risk-based currency momentum. The results
hold for forecast horizons of 3, 12, and 24 months, and they are robust after accounting
for transaction costs. The idiosyncratic component of currency expectations does not
contain important information for the cross-section of currency returns. Our strategy
is more significant for currencies with low sentiment and it is not driven by volatility
and illiquidity. The results are robust when we extract the systematic component of the
forecasts using a larger number of predictors.
JEL Classification: F31, G12, G15
Keywords: currency expectations, systematic risk, foreign exchange.

1 Introduction
Return expectations of investors in surveys play an important role in asset pricing as they
tendtodifferfromthoseimpliedbyrationalexpectationsmodels. GreenwoodandShleifer
(2014) document that equity return expectations highly correlate with past returns and
the market index. However, they negatively correlate with model-implied stock returns.
Early literature in foreign exchange also highlights that consensus forecasts of exchange
rates tend to be biased, offering limited information for future currency returns in the
short-run (e.g., Frankel and Froot, 1987; Froot and Frankel, 1989). However, analysts’
forecasts are more accurate for longer horizons. We show that the systematic component
of analysts’ forecasts contains important information for future currency returns for long
and short horizons.
Our paper contributes to the vast literature on the foreign exchange market that
studies the information content of analyst forecasts. In particular, we are the first to
showstrongevidenceof short-termpredictabilityofthesystematiccomponentofanalysts’
forecasts. We also find evidence of long-term cross-sectional predictability. Our results
demonstrate that analysts can very well predict global risks, but they are not able to
predict the country-specific component of currency returns. Thus, only the systematic
component of analyst expectations contains important information for both short and
long-term movement of currency returns.
Our findings are in line with Kremens, Martin, and Varela (2023), who investigate
exchangerateexpectationsinsurveysofprofessionalinvestorsandshowthattheysuccess-
fully predict currency appreciation at thetwo-year horizon. However, they find that sur-
vey expectations are less successful in predicting exchange rate appreciation over shorter
horizons. Thus, our paper differs in that we show that the systematic component of
analyst forecasts demonstrates strong predictability over both short and long horizons.
Kremens, Martin, and Varela (2023) also highlight that there is no “secret sauce” in ex-
pectations. Specifically, they show that the risk-neutral covariance between the exchange

rate and the equity market, the real exchange rate, and the current account relative to
GDP explain most of their variation. We show that the systematic component of surveys
that is computed using a small number of predictors offers strong short and long-term
predictability of the cross-section of currency returns.
First, we calculate the systematic component of currency returns based on a predictive
Fama and MacBeth (1973) regression of currency expectations on lagged characteristics
or risk factors. To facilitate interpretation and obtain a parsimonious model, our set of
predictors includes forward discounts, momentum, and value. The choice of the variables
is motivated by the Nucera, Sarno, and Zinna (2023), who show that only these three
variables are strong predictors of currency returns. Chernov, Dahlquist, and Lochstoer
(2023) also use a similar set of variables. The systematic component of the currency
expectations is defined as the prediction of this regression. We label this variable as a
risk-based appreciation expectation.
Second, we examine the cross-sectional predictive ability of the forecasts. To this end,
we sort currencies into quintiles based on their risk-based appreciation expectation every
month. We consider forecast horizons of three and twelve months. Our portfolios have
formation periods of one month and holding periods of 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. We find
that the risk component of analyst forecasts is a strong predictor of the cross-section
of currency returns for both forecast horizons. The predictability is strong for different
holding periods. Specifically, we find that a long-short strategy that buys currencies
with high risk-based appreciation expectations and sells currencies with low risk-based
appreciation expectations offers positive and statistically significant currency excess re-
turns. We decompose the currency excess return of the strategy into forward discounts
and exchange rate changes. We find strong evidence of exchange rate predictability. The
residual component does not provide significant currency returns.
We also consider the implementation cost of the strategy, and we find that it offers
highly significant currency excess returns, regardless of the holding period or the forecast
2

horizon. We find that the results are stronger for shorter holding periods, while there are
no significant differences between the forecast horizons of 3 and 12 months. To guard
against the tradability concerns of the currencies that we consider, we also show results for
a group of more liquid currencies. Specifically, we construct portfolios of G10 currencies
and find the spread portfolios offer positive and statistically significant returns before and
after transaction costs.
The strategy offers Sharpe ratios that range from 0.32 to 0.67, and the results are
similar for 3 and 12-month forecast horizons. We also calculate the alphas of our strate-
gies with respect to other asset pricing models. In particular, we contemporaneously
regress the excess return of the risk-based appreciation expectation factor of the dollar
(DOL) and carry trade (HML) factor of Lustig, Roussanov, and Verdelhan (2011) and
find positive and statistically significant alphas. We perform the same exercise for as-
set pricing models that include the dollar factors as well as value or momentum. The
models offer alphas that are positive and statistically significant. We find similar results
when we include the dollar, carry, value, and momentum in our model. The choice of
the forecast horizon does not alter our results. We also run a Fama and MacBeth (1973)
regression of currency excess returns or exchange rate changes on the risk-based currency
appreciation expectation variable as well as momentum, value, and carry trade, and we
find strong evidence of short-term and long-term predictability. The results are highly
significant across different holding periods. They are also similar for 3 and 12-month
forecast horizons.
In our main analysis, we extract the systematic component of currency expectations
using only three predictors, namely, forward discounts, value and momentum. How-
ever, one could argue that we might omit important information, and that we do not
estimate the systematic component with precision. To this end, we employ a larger num-
ber of predictors that include volatility, illiquidity, the dividend price ratio (DP), the
price-to-earnings ratio (PE), the inflation rate (INF), the unemployment rate (UN), and
3

the market capitalization (MKTCAP). Our original variables are also included in the
extended model. To guard against overfitting, we estimate the model via the Elastic
Net (ENet). We find that our results are similar when we include a larger number of
predictors, but they are not improved in comparison to our baseline model. This is in
line with the literature, which highlights that our initial predictors can explain most of
the variation of currency returns (e.g., Nucera, Sarno, and Zinna, 2023). This is further
verified when we compute the variable importance of the ENet model. We observe that
the original three variables–forward discounts, value and momentum–dominate. We also
find that volatility is an important predictor.
We also investigate whether the trading activity of market participants could explain
our results in some measure. To this end, we contemporaneously sort currency order
imbalances based on the risk-based currency appreciation expectation variable. We show
results for the buy versus sell-side as well for corporates, funds, and non-bank financials.
We find that the difference between high and low risk-based currency expectation port-
folios is significant for the buy vs. sell-side and corporates. We verify this result in Fama
and MacBeth (1973) regressions with controls. We do not find significant results for
funds and non-bank financials. We perform the same exercise with trading volume and
find that the trading activity of corporates and funds could partly explain our findings.
We find similar results when we sort based on lagged risk-based currency expectations.
Following Filippou et al. (2023), we calculate the media sentiment for different currencies
and find that our strategy is more profitable for currencies with low sentiment. This is
not surprising as investors require a risk premium for currencies with negative sentiment.
Our results are robust to a a battery of different tests. Specifically, our results are
unaffected when we sort currencies into portfolios based on two lags of the risk-based
currency appreciation measure. We find similar results for 24-month forecast horizons.
The results are similar for rank-weighted portfolios. The results are also robust when we
calculate the systematic component of currency expectations based on a pooled regression
4

with a large number of predictors or when we consider alternative specifications of the
measure. We also find in double sorts that volatility and illiquidity do not drive our
results.
Our work is based on survey expectations fromConsensus Economics. There are sev-
eral papers in the literature that use the same surveys. Specifically, Stavrakeva and Tang
(2020) and De Marco, Macchiavelli, and Valchev (2022) find that these forecasts are in
line with market participants’ positioning in over-the-counter foreign exchange markets
and sovereign bond markets. Kalemli-Ozcan and Varela (2021) examine forecast errors
in advanced and emerging markets and their relationship with UIP deviations. Candian
and De Leo (2023) employ these forecasts to build a model of under- and overreaction
to interest rates. They show that the predictable currency forecast errors could partly
explain the uncovered-interest-parity (UIP) puzzle and its reversal at longer horizons.
Della Corte, Gao, and Jeanneret (2023) use the relationship betweenConsensus expec-
tations and exchange rate premia computed from option prices to examine how the term
structure of risk preferences varies across economic conditions. Kremens, Martin, and
Varela (2023) examine both the information that is and is not included in both quantos
and surveys and assess their predictive ability for realized exchange rate changes. Beck-
mann and Reitz (2023) show that exchange rate Consensus forecasts correlate strongly
with the quanto-implied risk premium. They also show that the quanto-implied risk
premium serves as a good proxy for intermediary capital ratios. Nagel and Xu (2023)
employ forecast horizons for up to one year and show that expected returns from surveys
are less cyclical than “objective” risk premia that are based on in-sample regressions.
Using Blue Chip Financial Forecasts, Dahlquist and Söderlind (2023) examine the ex-
pectations of individual forecasters in the foreign exchange market. They find that the
survey risk premium is less countercyclical than the rational risk premium and that fore-
casters learn from their own forecast errors and not from consensus forecast errors. Thus,
they highlight the importance of individual exchange rate forecasts.

2 Data and Portfolio Construction
In this section, we describe the data and portfolio construction.
Spot and Forward Exchange Rates. We obtain daily spot, 1-month, 3-month, 6-
month and 12-month forward exchange rates against the U.S. dollar from Barclays and
Reuters via Datastream/Refinitiv. The data span the period from October 1990 to Oc-
tober 2016. In the main analysis, we include mid quotes that are defined as the mean
of the bid and ask quotes for each currency. We control for transaction costs as a ro-
bustness check. Following Burnside, Eichenbaum, Kleshchelski, and Rebelo (2011), we
construct the end-of-month series using the exchange rate on the last trading day within
the month. Thus, the exchange rates are not averaged over each month, resembling an
investor’s behavior in real time.
The sample consists of the following 34 countries: Australia, Brazil, Bulgaria, Canada,
Croatia, Cyprus, Czech Republic, Denmark, Egypt, Euro, Hong Kong, Hungary, India,
Indonesia, Israel, Japan, Malaysia, Mexico, New Zealand, Norway, Philippines, Poland,
Russia, Saudi Arabia, Singapore, Slovakia, South Africa, South Korea, Sweden, Switzer-
land, Taiwan, Thailand, Ukraine, United Kingdom.1
In order to account for tradability concerns and make our analysis more robust, we
also consider a smaller subsample of more liquid currencies. This group is a subset of
the first and includes G10 currencies. This group includes: Australia, Canada, the Euro
Area, Germany (replaced by the euro in 1999), Japan, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden,
Switzerland, and the United Kingdom. The tenth currency is the U.S. dollar itself, which

Analysts’forecasts. Followingpreviousstudies(e.g.,Kalemli-OzcanandVarela,2021;
Stavrakeva and Tang, 2020), we employ analysts’ forecasts from Consensus Economics to

exchange rate movement over 3, 12, and 24-month horizons. They make the consensus
forecasts available in the middle of each month.

Currency Risk-based Expectation Portfolios. At the end of each month t, we
allocate currencies into quintiles based on the risk component of the currency forecast
(– cAE). The first portfolio contains low-risk expectation currencies, and the last consists
of high-risk expectation currencies. The currency excess returns within each portfolio are
equally weighted. Thus, we build a zero-cost risk expectation portfolio (HML) that buys
high-risk expectation currencies while short-selling low-risk expectation currencies.
Currency Carry Trade Portfolios. At the end of each montht, we allocate curren-
cies into quintiles based on their forward discounts (ft – st). The first portfolio contains
currencies with low interest rates, and the last portfolio consists of high-interest-rate cur-
rencies. The currency excess returns within each portfolio are equally weighted. Thus,
we build a zero-cost carry-trade portfolio (CAR) that buys investment currencies while
short-selling funding currencies.
Currency Momentum Portfolios. At the end of each montht, we allocate currencies
into quintiles based on their return. The first portfolio contains countries with poor past
performances (losers), and the last portfolio consists of past winners. The currency excess
returns within each portfolio are equally weighted. Thus, we build a zero-cost momentum
portfolio (MOM) that buys winner currencies while short-selling loser currencies.
Currency Value Portfolios. At the end of each montht, we allocate currencies into
quintiles based on their real exchange rate return. The first portfolio contains overvalued
currencies, and the last comprises undervalued ones. The currency excess returns within
each portfolio are equally weighted. Thus, we build a zero-cost value portfolio (VAL)
that buys undervalued currencies while short-selling overvalued currencies.

3 Empirical Results
In this section, we construct the risk-based currency expectation measure and present
the empirical analysis for their asset pricing implication.
3.1 Construction of Risk-based Appreciation Expectations

the foreign currency based on the analysts’ forecast consensus. Specifically, we calculate
the expected currency return that is defined as the difference between the forecast for the
log spot exchange rate at timet for time t + 3minus the realized log spot exchange rate
at time t. Thus, the appreciation expectation (AE) is:
AEi ,t|t+3 = ˜si ,t|t+3 – si ,t , (3)

we define the dependent variableAEi ,t|t+3 as appreciation expectation according to its
economic interpretation.4

of currency returns in the literature (e.g., Chernov, Dahlquist, and Lochstoer, 2023). In
this way, our results offer a strong economic interpretation of the source of risk that drives
analysts’ expectations and contains important information for future currency returns.

results of Fama and MacBeth (1973) regressions, which control for other currency char-

predictor of future currency returns, even after accounting for other factors that explain
the cross-section of currency returns.
3.4 Estimation of Risk-based Expectations via Machine Learning
In our main analysis, we extract the systematic component of currency expectations
using only forward discounts, momentum, and value predictors. In this way, we facilitate
the interpretation of the results and focus on a parsimonious model that incorporates
the most successful predictors of the cross-section of currency returns (e.g., Chernov,
Dahlquist, and Lochstoer, 2023; Nucera, Sarno, and Zinna, 2023). In this section, we
consider a plethora of predictors and employ machine learning techniques to estimate the
model to guard against overfitting. It is also well documented in the literature that a
richer information set is needed to extract the systematic component of returns (e.g., Li,
Yuan, and Zhou, 2023; Filippou, Li, Yuan, and Zhou, 2023; Beckmeyer, Filippou, and
Zhou, 2023). Specifically, we apply Elastic Net to estimate the coefficients of our model.

6 Conclusions
We examine the information content of currency expectations for the cross-section of
currency returns. In line with the literature, we find that analysts are able to forecast
currency returns in the long run, but they are not successful in shorter horizons. How-
ever, we find that the systematic component of analysts’ forecasts can forecast exchange
rate fluctuations in the short run, while the idiosyncratic component does not contain
important information for the cross-section of currency returns.
We run a Fama and MacBeth (1973) regression of currency expectations on forward
discounts, value, and momentum. The prediction of this regression is the systematic
component of currency expectations. We denote this measure as risk-based currency
appreciation. We allocate currencies into portfolios based on this measure and find that
it renders highly significant currency excess returns and Sharpe ratios. We find similar
results for holding periods of 1 to 12 months and a formation period of one month. The
results are also similar for forecast horizons of 3 and 12 months.
The returns of the strategy remain sizable after accounting for transaction costs. The
results are robust to more liquid currencies. Specifically, we find similar results for G10
currencies. We also find that risk-based appreciation expectation variables are a strong
predictor of currency returns even after controlling for other factors. We also find similar
results when we estimate the systematic component of currency expectations with a larger
number of predictors.
We also explore the mechanism that could drive our results. We find that order
imbalances of buy versus sell side and corporates could partly explain the results. We
also find that the trading volume of corporates and funds is negatively related to risk-
based currency appreciation. Thus, we find that analysts’ forecasts are more successful
40

in the long run, but only the systematic component of currency expectations contains
important information for the cross-section of currency returns over short horizons.
